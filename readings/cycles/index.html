Working with cycles.

Give an intention the amount of time it actually needs.

Some things need the next hour. Others need a week, or a change of season. The app uses cycles to keep those different scales in view. Some describe biological timing; others are symbolic calendars for reflection. The app does not measure your personal biological rhythms or predict your mood.

Its core intention practice works with four rhythms: ultradian, circadian, septenary and lunar. The wider reading room includes seasonal and annual perspectives, with names such as Season of Awakening, Time of Light, Season of Harvest and The Quieting.

The next few hours

The app calls this the ultradian intention. In biology, ultradian rhythms repeat within a day. In this practice, the useful question is simpler: how much attention do you have available now, and what would you like to give it to?

Choose a task small enough to finish or meaningfully advance. If your attention has gone, the intention may need to be a break. Try a work period you can sustain, and notice when you need to stop.

What would you like to focus on in the next few hours?

Today

A circadian rhythm runs on roughly a 24-hour cycle. Light and dark help set the body’s daily timing. The app uses the day as a place to put an intention you can revisit before going to sleep.

A daily intention might hold several shorter actions together. It could also be something you want to remember while doing the ordinary things already on your calendar.

What is one thing you want to achieve by the end of today?

The week

Septenary means seven days. The weekly intention gives you a little distance from the urgency of today. A task that keeps slipping may need a place in the week, a smaller first step, or an honest decision to stop carrying it.

Review the week as a whole. What did you make room for, and what kept being crowded out?

Looking at the upcoming week, what intention will shape your actions?

The lunar cycle

The lunar frame offers a longer interval to return to a question. You might use it to begin something, notice what is developing, and review it later. It gives you a regular point at which to pause and review.

A longer intention can remain open while you change the smaller steps beneath it.

Seasons, including the quiet ones

The app’s seasonal names describe different attitudes to a period of life. Season of Awakening gives space to beginnings. Time of Light turns outward. Season of Harvest asks what you have learned and want to keep. The Quieting gives rest and review their own place.

Choose the image that fits the period you are actually living through, wherever you are and whatever the season outside. Rest and review deserve time of their own.

Try it on paper

Write one intention for today and one for the week. Under each, write the next action you can actually take. Come back tomorrow. Keep what still makes sense; change what does not.

These notes adapt the app’s Cycles knowledge base and intention questions. Biological timing and symbolic planning frames are kept distinct. For the biological account of daily rhythms, see the National Institute of General Medical Sciences’ circadian rhythms overview.
